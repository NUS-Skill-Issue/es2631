# Elements of Thought

An element identifies what part of the reasoning is being examined. A standard identifies how its quality is being judged. A purpose can be clear but unimportant. Information can be accurate but irrelevant to the question.

| Element | Identify it by asking |
|---|---|
| Purpose | What is the reasoning trying to achieve? |
| Question at issue | What problem is it trying to answer? |
| Point of view | From whose perspective is the problem framed? |
| Information | What observations, data or testimony are supplied? |
| Interpretation and inference | What conclusion is drawn from the information? |
| Concepts | Which ideas, definitions or models structure the argument? |
| Assumptions | What must be accepted for the inference to work? |
| Implications and consequences | What follows if the conclusion is accepted or acted upon? |

## Build a small reasoning map

For each element relevant to the task, record the source passage or slide, what it says and whether the interpretation is yours. A full map can help with a long critique. A focused question may need only one or two rows.

An unstated assumption is an inference about the reasoning. Explain what makes that assumption necessary. Do not invent the author's motives or claim to know an absent stakeholder's views.

If a deck omits maintenance cost, say that the deck does not report it. This does not establish that the team ignored maintenance in its actual work. Explain whether the omission prevents the reader from evaluating the conclusion.

## Useful pairings

These are examples rather than required assignments between standards and elements.

| Standard | Possible element | What to examine |
|---|---|---|
| Clarity | Purpose or concepts | Is the objective or term understandable? |
| Accuracy | Information | Is the reported evidence correct and verifiable? |
| Precision | Question or information | Are boundaries, measurements and units sufficiently specific? |
| Relevance | Information | Does the evidence bear on the question? |
| Depth | Question or implications | Are the difficult conditions and dependencies addressed? |
| Breadth | Point of view or inference | Are materially different perspectives or explanations considered? |
| Logic | Inference or assumptions | Does the conclusion follow under the stated conditions? |
| Significance | Purpose or implications | Is attention directed to what affects the decision most? |
| Fairness | Point of view or assumptions | Are competing interests and claims represented fairly? |

The same element can be tested by several standards. Information might be accurate, imprecise and irrelevant at the same time. Avoid treating coverage as a box ticking exercise. Cover all eight elements only when the task requires that breadth.

Use plain names in prose where helpful. A sentence such as “The conclusion assumes that the trial conditions will persist” identifies both the inference and its assumption without explaining the entire framework again.
